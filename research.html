Current research

My current research at the University of Graz explores mineral exploration in Europe in the context of the energy transition, with case studies in Austria. I examine how geologists, companies, investors, public authorities and local communities shape whether potential mineral resources become viable mining projects. My focus is on the early stages of exploration, a process marked by uncertainty and anticipation.

My focus on Europe responds to a significant moment in its mineral supply policies. The EU Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA), which entered into force in 2024, places secure access to these materials firmly on the political agenda. It seeks to reduce reliance on external suppliers by strengthening extraction, processing and recycling within the EU, while diversifying sources of supply.

But how are these ambitions experienced locally? My research asks how the rush to secure minerals for the energy transition shapes places, relationships and possible futures in regions where exploration takes place.

Mineral exploration brings together actors across local, regional, national and global scales. Their interactions generate different expectations and visions for a region’s future. I pay particular attention to geologists, who are often among the first company representatives to work on the ground and engage informally with local people. As intermediaries between rocks and people, they interpret geological potential while also helping to shape perceptions of the company they represent.

These encounters happen in places shaped by local histories and regional identities, which in turn influence how exploration develops. Local responses can therefore vary considerably, even when projects are carried out by the same company. I understand an exploration site in broader terms, not just as a physical location where mineral resources are assessed but also as a space shaped by these interactions across scales. Crucially, my research also asks how these actors enter this space, given their unequal access to knowledge, different levels of authority and unequal capacity to influence decisions.
